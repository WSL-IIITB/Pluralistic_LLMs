# **Secondary-School Dropout in Karnataka: A District-by-District Analysis**

The secondary education landscape in Karnataka exhibits a complex interplay of socio-economic distress, infrastructural deficits, and systemic governance failures that drive students out of the classroom between Classes 8 and 10\. While the state has successfully reduced lower primary dropout rates to near zero, the high school dropout rate has spiked alarmingly, reaching 22.1% in 2023–24 before slightly correcting to 18.3% in 2024–251. The crisis disproportionately affects marginalized communities, with Scheduled Caste (SC) and Scheduled Tribe (ST) students experiencing secondary dropout rates of 31.9% and 24.1%, respectively1. Furthermore, Karnataka's female secondary dropout rate stands at 13.9%, the highest among major Indian states2.  
This report provides an exhaustive district-by-district analysis of the 15 specified districts across the Old Mysuru, Bayaluseeme, and Malnad regions. The analysis unpacks the localized drivers of secondary school abandonment, ranging from the systemic artificial deflation of SSLC performance metrics to deep-rooted child marriage and seasonal migration patterns.

## **Bengaluru Urban**

The dropout crisis in Bengaluru Urban is defined by the severe economic pressures of a rapidly expanding metropolitan economy and the massive, untracked influx of migrant labour. The high cost of living forces impoverished adolescents to abandon high school and enter the unorganized retail and garment sectors to supplement household incomes4. The transition from upper primary to secondary school acts as a primary exit point; survey data indicates that 63% of dropouts occur between ages 11 and 13 as students complete lower classes but face insurmountable access barriers to high schools6.  
Compounding these economic realities are severe infrastructural failures. Despite its status as a major IT hub, judicial audits by the High Court recently exposed that several government schools in the district—specifically one in Bengaluru North and four in Bengaluru South—operate without any toilet facilities7. This lack of basic sanitation serves as a direct catalyst for the dropout of adolescent girls post-puberty. Furthermore, adolescent mental health crises, including general anxiety and depression, account for 4.1% of local health cases, further alienating vulnerable youth from the rigorous SSLC academic environment8.

## **Bengaluru Rural**

Bengaluru Rural experiences substantial secondary dropout rates fueled heavily by child labour and severe sanitation deficits. Proximity to the urban metropolis creates high demand for cheap labour, drawing adolescents into long hours of unorganized work. Labour department sweeps have frequently targeted the district, noting that child labour incidence remains stubbornly high even in the post-pandemic recovery phase, with 145 cases recorded over a three-year window11.  
The systemic failure to retain students is exacerbated by abysmal school infrastructure. Similar to its urban counterpart, Bengaluru Rural was flagged in High Court proceedings for having five schools completely devoid of drinking water and toilet infrastructure, a known absolute barrier to female secondary school retention7. Despite these acute challenges, the district manages a moderate SSLC performance, though child rights groups continually express concern over thousands of post-COVID "missing children" who never returned to the education department's tracking systems11.

## **Ramanagara**

Secondary school dropouts in Ramanagara are predominantly driven by child marriage and economic distress. Despite the district's relative prosperity and geographic proximity to Bengaluru, traditional gender norms forcefully curtail female education. State child welfare data identifies Ramanagara as one of the top southern districts for child marriage, officially recording 44 cases between 2020 and 202312. Once married, adolescent girls are immediately withdrawn from the SSLC pipeline.  
Simultaneously, the district is grappling with a noted rise in child labour incidents, pulling male students out of classrooms to support family finances11. The educational tracking in the district remains event-driven rather than statistical, relying on reactive labour rescues and child marriage interventions rather than proactive bridge courses for slow learners transitioning into Class 9\.

## **Mandya**

Mandya presents a deeply concerning paradox: despite its reputation for agricultural wealth in the Old Mysuru region, it systematically fails to retain adolescent girls in secondary schools due to aggressively entrenched gender biases. Data from the Women and Child Welfare Department reveals that Mandya recorded 172 child marriages from 2020 to 2023, making it the highest in the entire state12. This socio-cultural devaluing of female education is further underscored by concurrent alarming surges in female foeticide12.  
Furthermore, educational authorities pinpoint the extreme financial vulnerability of single-parent households as a primary driver of irregular attendance and eventual dropout14. Without a dual income, single parents frequently rely on their adolescent children to work or manage domestic duties. While the district achieved a stellar 96.7% SSLC pass rate in 20239, this metric masks the reality of the highly vulnerable students who are filtered out of the system long before they reach the examination hall. Recent intensive interventions by the District Child Protection Officer have begun to curb child marriages, reporting a drop to 25 cases by late 202515.

## **Mysuru**

Secondary student retention in Mysuru is severely compromised by underage marriages and the acute poverty of single-parent households. In just a six-month window from April to September 2024, Mysuru reported 105 child marriage complaints—the highest in the state—resulting in 36 executed marriages that permanently stripped girls of their right to high school education16. The crisis has grown so severe that the state government instituted a ₹50,000 reward for gram panchayats that successfully prevent the practice16.  
Economic strain acts as the secondary pillar of the dropout crisis. Single-parent families, particularly those relying on seasonal or unorganized labour, frequently pull their children out of school to supplement income14. Educational officials in Mysuru have publicly lamented the total absence of trained psychological counsellors in government high schools, leaving teachers unable to appropriately intervene when traumatized or impoverished students begin exhibiting chronic absenteeism14. To combat the intersecting issues of dropout and exploitation, the district administration is currently attempting to establish dedicated residential schools for rescued child labourers17.

## **Chamarajanagara**

The dropout crisis in Chamarajanagara is intrinsically tied to its unique demographic composition, which features a vast and highly vulnerable tribal population. State demographic and educational surveys explicitly blame deeply rooted tribal social customs for the premature termination of secondary education, particularly for girls14. Formal schooling is frequently superseded by traditional lifestyle choices and early marriage, though aggressive enforcement by the state restricted reported child marriages to merely three instances in the first ten months of 202515.  
Additionally, the district suffers from high rates of economic migration. Landless tribal families and single parents routinely migrate across district borders for seasonal agricultural labour. Adolescents in these families are forced to abandon their SSLC preparations to accompany their parents, contributing to continuous, invisible attrition from the school system14.

## **Chikkaballapur**

Chikkaballapur suffers from catastrophic structural transition bottlenecks and severe physical security threats to adolescent girls. Educational authorities acknowledge a staggering 35% to 40% dropout rate occurring precisely at the transition between Class 10 and Class 1218. This is driven by an acute geographic shortage of accessible high schools and pre-university (PU) colleges in rural belts, physically preventing students from continuing their education18.  
Furthermore, extreme poverty catalyzes a brutal child marriage economy. Adolescent girls are frequently pulled from Class 9 or 10 and forced into marriages, with documented instances of 15- and 17-year-old girls being abducted by older men from neighboring states or forced into wedlock while their parents are absent19. Academically, the district is plagued by governance failures, including the suspension of teachers for aiding SSLC malpractice and the circulation of question papers via WhatsApp, which deeply undermines the integrity of the secondary examination system21.

## **Kolar**

Secondary education in Kolar is devastated by systemic neglect, acute teacher shortages, and geographic isolation. The severity of the infrastructure crisis is exemplified by a historic 126-year-old government school in Kolar Gold Fields (KGF), which recorded a shocking 0% SSLC pass rate in 202423. All 38 students failed because the school operated with only four teachers for 86 high school students, entirely lacking subject teachers for Science and Hindi23.  
Outside the classroom, extreme poverty and the physical distance to secondary institutions cripple retention. The Right to Education Act guarantees free schooling only until Class 8\. Following this, girls in rural Kolar are frequently forced to drop out because they cannot afford the ₹30 daily bus fare to reach distant high schools, nor do government buses reliably service remote villages24. This systemic poverty also perpetuates child marriage, pulling both the adolescent brides and their older siblings out of school to manage household labor25.

## **Tumakuru**

Tumakuru's secondary education pipeline is obstructed by extreme financial constraints and severe localized child labour exploitation. Financial inability to sustain schooling costs directly impacts student survival; in a tragic instance, an SSLC girl student attempted suicide because her family could not afford the examination fees26.  
The district also grapples with organized child exploitation. Police recently registered cases against madrasa management in Tumakuru Rural after 24 runaway children alleged severe abuse and forced labour, stripping them of any formal educational rights27. Historic data indicates sub-areas like Madhugiri struggle significantly with retention, seeing hundreds of students exit the system to engage in unorganized labour28.

While rural students in the district are often cited by educators as highly focused and aware of their parents' financial struggles, the district’s overall SSLC performance has shown volatility, prompting direct warnings from the Chief Minister to local teachers to improve academic outcomes30. ## **Shivamogga**

Secondary school attrition in Shivamogga is a complex mix of systemic administrative fraud, safety concerns in residential hostels, and child marriage. In a massive governance failure, government schools in the district were caught forcing academically weaker students to register for the SSLC exams as "private candidates" rather than regular students33. This malicious practice is designed to artificially inflate the school's official pass percentage. Consequently, these students are deprived of their 20 internal assessment marks, leading to mass failures. For instance, out of 19,796 private candidates statewide, the pass percentage was a dismal 6.45%33. Once failed, these students are highly likely to drop out permanently.  
Additionally, the State Commission for Protection of Child Rights noted a high rate of dropouts specifically among marginalized students residing in Social Welfare Department SC/ST hostels, citing unlivable environments and poor food quality34. Child marriage also remains a persistent threat, with 79 cases recorded in 2023–24, though recent police coordination with the education department aims to track and return dropped-out youth to classrooms35.

## **Chikkamagaluru**

The agricultural economy of the Malnad region dictates the educational outcomes in Chikkamagaluru. Secondary students suffer from acute seasonal migration; hundreds of families migrate to the district to work in coffee and pepper plantations during the harvest season17. Adolescent children are pulled out of school in their home districts and spend months living in makeshift roadside tents, acting as daytime caregivers for younger siblings while their parents harvest crops17.  
Like Shivamogga, Chikkamagaluru is heavily implicated in the SSLC "private candidate" scam. At Karnataka Public School (KPS) Begar, administrators secretly registered regular students as private candidates without parental consent to protect the school's ranking, resulting in the students failing the exam and exiting the education system33. The district is also navigating socio-religious access gaps; the hijab controversy saw parents threatening to withdraw their adolescent daughters from high schools if headscarves were banned, introducing a massive social barrier to continuous secondary education for minority girls40.

## **Kodagu**

Kodagu's secondary dropout factors are intrinsically linked to its vast coffee plantation landscape and the socioeconomic vulnerabilities of the estate worker population. The physical environment poses direct safety threats; traversing remote plantations to reach high schools is perilous, highlighted by frequent fatal elephant trampling incidents41.  
Furthermore, child marriage aggressively curtails secondary education. In a highly publicized tragedy, a 16-year-old girl—the only student from a remote Kodagu village school to pass the SSLC exam—was immediately forced into an engagement, resulting in her subsequent murder following a tip-off to the police42. Such severe social pressures ensure that even academically successful adolescent girls are violently removed from the higher education pipeline. Despite these severe localized issues, the district manages a robust overall SSLC pass rate of 82.21%38.

## **Hassan**

Hassan faces a severe and escalating child marriage crisis that devastates female secondary school retention. Recent data revealed that child marriages in the district doubled over a two-year period, placing Hassan at the absolute top of the state for this social evil44. This is driven by deep-rooted poverty and the economic strain on single-parent households, who routinely withdraw adolescents from school to marry them off or send them to work14.  
The district struggles with a high absolute number of out-of-school children, with surveys identifying 1,819 truant youths, heavily concentrated in specific taluks46. In many cases, minor girls are kidnapped under the guise of love affairs and forced into marriages, creating complex legal and social barriers for child welfare officers trying to return them to classrooms45. To combat this, local police have formed specialized all-women squads ("Hasanamba Pade") to track dropouts and prevent underage marriages45.
