# **Comprehensive Analysis of Secondary-School Dropouts in Karnataka: A District-by-District Evaluation**

## **Introduction**

The transition through secondary education—specifically spanning Classes 8 through 10 and culminating in the Secondary School Leaving Certificate (SSLC) examinations—represents a critical vulnerability in Karnataka’s educational architecture. While the state frequently points to improving overall enrolment and infrastructure metrics, a granular, district-by-district analysis reveals a highly fragmented landscape. Secondary school attrition in Karnataka is not a monolithic issue; it is a complex tapestry of localized socio-economic crises, agrarian distress, deeply entrenched gender norms, and bureaucratic inefficiencies.  
In the high-performing coastal districts of Dakshina Kannada and Udupi, near-perfect SSLC pass rates mask the quiet disappearance of migrant labourers' children. Conversely, in the Kalyana Karnataka and Kitturu Karnataka regions—encompassing districts like Kalaburagi, Raichur, Yadgir, and Koppal—systemic poverty drives severe, overt dropout mechanisms. Here, secondary education is aggressively undermined by the demands of cotton and sugarcane agriculture, bonded shepherding, and the exploitation of mass weddings to facilitate child marriages. Furthermore, institutional failures, ranging from the diversion of teaching staff for census duties to fraudulent "zero absentee" reporting by local education departments, exacerbate the crisis by blinding policymakers to the true scale of the problem.  
This report synthesizes regional news, non-governmental action research, and official state data to provide an exhaustive evaluation of secondary school dropouts across 15 targeted districts in Karnataka. Additionally, it tests the validity of official UIDAI/NITI Aayog statistical factors—specifically concerning sanitation infrastructure and English-medium instruction—against real-world, district-specific evidence.

## **Dakshina Kannada**

Dakshina Kannada consistently commands the apex of Karnataka's educational rankings, recording an impressive 98.40 percent SSLC pass rate and securing the first position in the state in 20261. The district's success is underpinned by rigorous academic monitoring, with local authorities deploying targeted workshops for teachers to coach slow learners and ensure 100 percent retention during the crucial Class 10 year2.  
However, this statistical dominance obscures specific, localized dropout vulnerabilities. The primary driver of secondary school attrition in Dakshina Kannada is the seasonal and distress migration of labourer families4. Moving frequently for work, migrant parents routinely pull their children from the formal schooling system. Surveys have historically identified these migration patterns as the leading cause of out-of-school children in the region, prompting local Sarva Shiksha Abhiyan coordinators to collaborate with the Child Welfare Commission and Childline to trace and reintegrate these students4.  
Furthermore, a subtle form of attrition manifests at the very end of the secondary cycle through exam absenteeism. Even within this highly disciplined academic environment, hundreds of enrolled students fail to appear for their final board examinations. In 2024, 329 students in the district were absent on the first day of the SSLC language papers, and similar trends continued into 2026, with 233 students missing their third-language examinations6. This late-stage absenteeism indicates that socio-economic pressures still manage to derail students right at the threshold of secondary completion.

## **Udupi**

Sharing the coastal educational belt with Dakshina Kannada, Udupi exhibits an almost identical demographic and academic profile. The district recorded a near-perfect SSLC pass percentage of 98.18 percent in 2026, retaining its position as the state's second-best-performing district9. The educational machinery in Udupi is highly efficient, with 51 schools recording a 100 percent pass rate in recent years8. To achieve these metrics, the Department of Public Instruction mandates intensive preparatory strategies, requiring teachers to isolate slow learners and drill them with question banks following their preparatory exams9.  
Despite these formidable retention mechanisms, Udupi is not immune to the final-hurdle dropouts seen in neighbouring districts. While mass structural dropouts in early secondary school (Class 8 and 9\) are rarely documented in mainstream reporting, silent attrition occurs via unexcused absences during the board exams. In the 2026 SSLC cycle, 62 students across blocks like Brahmavar, Karkala, and Byndoor failed to appear for their language papers7. Similarly, 122 students skipped core science papers in previous cycles10. These figures represent students who navigate the entirety of the secondary system only to exit without credentials, often due to sudden family crises, health issues, or undisclosed migration4.

## **Uttara Kannada**

Uttara Kannada presents a stark geographic and socio-economic contrast to its southern coastal neighbours. Characterized by dense forests, the Western Ghats, and remote tribal hamlets, the district faces unique geographical barriers to secondary education. Poverty acts as a severe structural deterrent, particularly within marginalized tribal groups such as the Siddi community. Young girls in these remote forest villages are frequently forced to drop out of school to assist their parents with daily wage labour, perpetuating a cycle of deep generational poverty11.  
The district also struggles with acute infrastructural and staffing deficits that degrade the quality of secondary education. Chronic vacancies for permanent teaching staff in high schools have forced the local administration to rely heavily on temporary guest teachers, destabilizing continuous mentorship for adolescent students13. Furthermore, external crises exert an outsized impact on this district; during the height of the COVID-19 pandemic, severe health anxieties prompted parents in the Sirsi sub-area to entirely withhold their children from attending SSLC mathematics and English exams14. Despite these compounded adversities, pockets of excellence remain, with multiple students from government schools in Sirsi scoring 624 out of 625 in the 2024 SSLC exams15.

## **Belagavi**

Belagavi’s secondary education system is deeply entwined with the region's massive sugarcane economy and prevailing patriarchal social norms. The district is a hotspot for child marriage, a practice that violently truncates the educational trajectory of adolescent girls. Case studies from the district reveal horrific systemic failures, such as a 15-year-old girl who was married off not once, but twice, before reaching the legal age of consent, resulting in severe trauma and the absolute termination of her schooling20. In response, state mechanisms like the 'Akka Pade' women's protection task force have been deployed, successfully intercepting child marriages—such as a recent case involving a 17-year-old girl destined for Maharashtra—and physically removing minors to District Child Protection Committees21.  
The local agrarian economy further destabilizes secondary retention. Belagavi is heavily reliant on sugarcane cultivation, a sector prone to intense labor demands and frequent, protracted farmer protests over the Fair and Remunerative Price (FRP) of crops22. Economic instability in agricultural households pushes adolescents out of classrooms and into the workforce. Authorities routinely conduct raids to rescue school-aged child labourers exploited by local commercial establishments, such as bakeries24. Despite these systemic socio-economic drains on the student body, the surviving cohort performs remarkably well, with the Belagavi educational division recording a 95.71 percent SSLC pass rate in 202625.

## **Vijayapura**

Secondary school dropouts in Vijayapura are driven by a convergence of grinding agricultural poverty, the exploitation of mass religious ceremonies, and profound administrative dysfunction within the educational department itself. The economic baseline of the district dictates that many students are children of daily-wage agricultural labourers, facing immense pressure to abandon their studies to support their families26. To bypass legal age restrictions, impoverished families frequently exploit mass wedding ceremonies to covertly marry off their school-aged daughters. The crisis has reached a point where the Deputy Commissioner had to mandate that organizers of mass weddings submit their intent 21 days in advance to allow authorities to verify the ages of the brides27.  
Compounding this socio-economic distress is a severe misallocation of educational resources. High school teachers in Vijayapura have publicly protested the government's practice of diverting them to non-academic duties, such as census data collection, immediately following the SSLC exams28. This administrative burden severely degrades teaching efficiency and evaluation quality. Historically, this has led to dismal SSLC performances, prompting the district in-charge minister to threaten disciplinary action against local education officials and send study teams to coastal districts to learn better retention methods29. The sheer desperation for secondary credentials in this depressed economy is evidenced by recent criminal cases where 42 candidates tampered with their old SSLC marks cards to secure employment in the Postal Department30.

## **Bagalkote**

Bagalkote grapples with a deeply entrenched gender-based dropout crisis, characterized by one of the most alarming concentrations of child marriages in the region. The sudden termination of secondary schooling for adolescent girls is an epidemic; local Child Development Project Officers (CDPOs) recently identified over 600 early-married girls within the district who are attempting to rebuild their lives after being forced out of the education system31. To combat this, grassroots interventions such as Children's Gram Sabhas are organized to give vulnerable youth a platform, and the state has authorized ₹50,000 cash rewards for gram panchayats that successfully intercept and prevent child marriages18.  
Social friction and economic hardship further complicate the district's educational landscape. Bagalkote was notably impacted by the statewide controversy over religious dress codes, where students chose to skip their final SSLC board examinations rather than comply with mandates to remove their hijabs, representing a direct, policy-driven cause of secondary school dropouts32. On the economic front, the necessity for teenagers to engage in agricultural labour to support family farms remains high33. This economic pressure to hold secondary credentials later in life frequently manifests as exam malpractice, with adults in their 30s and 40s attempting to clear the SSLC through illicit means to secure better wages and job designations34.

## **Dharwad**

Dharwad is actively battling structural disengagement among its secondary school populace. While the district has recently improved its state ranking in the SSLC exams, the raw passing percentage has ironically declined, indicating a system where failing students are simply dropping out rather than repeating the academic year35. Academic research conducted specifically in Dharwad’s rural areas attributes the high volume of upper-primary and early-secondary dropouts to a lethal combination of student disinterest, severe economic hardship, and the subsequent migration of families seeking wage labour37.  
To counter this deep-seated academic apathy, local authorities launched "Mission Vidyakashi," an aggressive initiative aimed at reforming teaching methodologies and bolstering student performance in SSLC and PU exams38. However, educational reforms cannot fully insulate students from the district's societal threats. The protection of adolescent girls remains a critical battleground; in the 2024-25 period alone, authorities successfully intercepted and prevented 28 child marriages, highlighting the persistent cultural pressure that threatens to pull girls out of secondary school permanently39.

## **Gadag**

The secondary education pipeline in Gadag is heavily compromised by severe agricultural poverty and the exploitation of mass social events. The district has historically struggled with academic retention, at one point sliding to the 34th educational position statewide with an abysmal 66.74 percent pass rate40. For young boys, poverty translates directly into bonded labour. Children as young as ten are frequently pulled from school and forced into shepherding, working as bonded labourers for half a decade without any access to middle or high school41.  
For girls in Gadag, the primary threat to secondary education is child marriage, which is systematically obscured through mass wedding ceremonies. These large-scale religious and social gatherings serve as a covert gateway for families to marry off underage girls, bypassing standard age verifications. This crisis forced child protection agencies to mandate age attestation on photographs to disrupt the practice42. The educational ecosystem is further destabilized by acute administrative and social turbulence; the district saw massive disruptions during the hijab controversy, culminating in the suspension of seven teachers and chief invigilators, which alienated vulnerable minority students during their critical SSLC examinations43.

## **Haveri**

Secondary school retention in Haveri is currently being strangled by a combination of rigid bureaucratic exclusion, degrading infrastructure, and acute social crises among adolescent girls. Economic hardship is frequently weaponized by private school managements; during times of financial distress, schools have routinely denied examination hall tickets to Class 10 students who are unable to clear their fee arrears. This bureaucratic exclusion directly forces students to miss their SSLC exams, effectively turning them into dropouts45. The rigidity of the system recently prompted a desperate SSLC student to sit for a mock exam in protest directly in front of the Deputy Commissioner's office after his headmaster maliciously withheld his hall ticket47.  
On the infrastructural front, the state government's proposals to close down or merge struggling government schools have sparked fierce resistance from student unions like the SFI, who argue that removing local schools will decimate secondary access for the poorest rural students48. More alarmingly, the Chairperson of the Karnataka State Commission for Women has flagged Haveri for highly concerning rates of child marriage and teenage pregnancy, biological and social events that irreversibly terminate the secondary education of young women in the district49.

## **Kalaburagi**

Kalaburagi represents the epicentre of Karnataka's secondary dropout crisis, characterized by devastating social evils and profound institutional data paralysis. The true scale of the crisis is heavily obscured by contradictory government data; a recent survey by the Rural Development and Panchayat Raj (RDPR) Department identified a staggering 11,137 out-of-school children, while the local Education Department claimed a mere 225 existed, suggesting massive administrative fraud or sheer incompetence in tracking dropouts51.  
The reality on the ground is grim. Kalaburagi records some of the highest child marriage rates in the state. In a single year, authorities prevented 128 child marriages52, and logged an additional 43 cases in just a four-month window53. The economic desperation driving this is so absolute that parents have been booked for marrying off their 15-year-old daughters simply because the children were ailing and the families could not afford medical care54. Child labour is equally rampant, with children dropping out of secondary school to survive via ragpicking or working in local scrap shops51. Consequently, Kalaburagi frequently languishes at the absolute bottom of the state's educational rankings, recording a dismal 85.06 percent SSLC pass rate in 2026, which ensures a steady stream of academic dropouts who fail the system entirely1.

## **Bidar**

In Bidar, the secondary education system is routinely decimated by the cultural and economic prioritization of early marriage over adolescent schooling. The district sees massive, concentrated spikes in child marriage clustering around religious festivals like Akshaya Tritiya. During these periods, authorities are forced to deploy special multi-departmental task forces and step up vigil at the grassroots level to prevent parents from permanently removing their daughters from Class 8 and 9 classrooms to be wed55.  
When students manage to stay in school, the pedagogical environment often fails them. Historically, Bidar has suffered from catastrophic SSLC failure rates, at one point seeing only 27 percent of students pass56. To address this systemic inability to educate adolescents, local NGOs and civil society groups have had to implement extreme remedial measures. Rotary Clubs in Bidar have recently established "Academic ICUs"—intensive, emergency bridge courses designed to catch out-of-school dropouts and desperately coach them to clear their SSLC examinations before they age out of the system at 1657.

## **Yadgir**

Yadgir suffers from a chronic and severe secondary dropout epidemic, driven primarily by the mass distress migration of agricultural labourer families. Unable to survive on the arid local lands, parents routinely migrate to urban centres like Bengaluru for construction and daily wage work, uprooting their children and violently severing their ties to formal education58.  
The scale of the crisis is immense. A recent door-to-door survey conducted by the Education Department identified 1,747 children specifically in the 14–18 age group who were entirely out of school59. Previous tracking data revealed that Yadgir had the highest number of chronic absentee students in the state, with 1,547 children missing classes for over three consecutive weeks60. The situation demands intense, unconventional interventions; the district administration utilizes National Institute of Open Schooling (NIOS) and 'Mitra' programmes to mainstream these older adolescents, and the crisis is so acute that the Deputy Commissioner has resorted to personally driving through villages to load dropouts into her vehicle and return them to classrooms59. Independent action research by the Azim Premji Foundation further corroborates that the transition into secondary school in Yadgir is severely bottlenecked by these deep-rooted economic constraints62.

## **Raichur**

Raichur’s secondary education system is hollowed out by the ravenous demand for child labour in the region's agricultural sector, particularly in cotton farming. Harvesting and pollinating Bt Cotton requires nimble fingers, leading landowners to aggressively recruit children, effectively emptying middle and high school classrooms. The Labour Department’s rescue operations underscore the massive scale of this exploitation; in a single year, authorities rescued 1,290 child labourers from cotton fields across the district, denying bail to employers who engaged in these practices63. The judiciary itself has issued severe warnings that agricultural child labour is destroying the academic futures of Raichur's youth64.  
Simultaneously, adolescent girls in Raichur face the dual threats of child marriage and related sexual violence, with recent high-profile cases involving 10 individuals booked under POCSO for marrying and assaulting a minor65. Compounding these tragedies is the profound administrative opacity of the local government. Despite older surveys identifying over 12,000 out-of-school children, the local Education Department recently submitted reports claiming "zero" absentees. This fraudulent data severely hampered reintegration efforts and drew intense outrage from the Karnataka State Commission for Protection of Child Rights, which demanded an immediate resurvey of the district17.

## **Koppal**

Koppal presents a uniquely tragic profile within Karnataka’s dropout landscape due to the grim persistence of the outlawed Devadasi system. Driven by extreme poverty and insurmountable medical debts, families in remote villages still dedicate their adolescent daughters to temple deities, a practice that initiates a life of sexual exploitation and guarantees the absolute termination of the child's formal education67. Recent surveys suggest there are over 3,600 Devadasis in the district, indicating a systemic, generational mechanism that continues to pull vulnerable girls out of secondary school69.  
Beyond the Devadasi system, standard child marriage is rampant and facilitated by institutional forgery. Authorities recently discovered that families were utilizing fabricated and forged school age certificates to successfully marry off minor girls during mass wedding ceremonies70. For boys, the primary threat to secondary enrollment is agricultural bonded labour. The broader region utilizes over 14,000 children in the agricultural sector, with young boys frequently pulled from school to serve as bonded shepherds for years at a time41.

Ballari, alongside the newly bifurcated district of Vijayanagara, struggles with secondary retention due to the deep integration of children into the regional mining and agricultural economies. High baseline poverty ensures that secondary education is viewed as an unaffordable luxury when compared to the immediate necessity of wage labour72.  
The region is also a well-known hotspot for mass wedding ceremonies, which are frequently weaponized by impoverished families to bypass state age-verification mechanisms and secretly marry off school-aged girls. This practice acts as a covert but highly effective pipeline, draining adolescent girls from the education system42. While local authorities attempt to track and rescue these students, institutional friction results in poor success rates. ## **Conclusion**

The analysis of secondary school dropouts across these 15 Karnataka districts reveals that educational attrition is fundamentally a symptom of broader socio-economic and institutional failures. While UIDAI and NITI Aayog statistical models frequently highlight infrastructure metrics like "improved sanitation" or "English medium instruction," the granular, district-level evidence gathered here overwhelmingly contradicts or ignores these factors. Instead, the real-world drivers of secondary dropouts are starkly economic and cultural.  
In the coastal districts, attrition is driven by the invisible movement of migrant labour. In the northern and north-eastern districts, the education system is actively cannibalized by the agricultural sector's demand for cheap child labour (cotton and sugarcane) and deeply entrenched patriarchal norms that prioritize child marriage and the Devadasi system over female education. Furthermore, the state's own administrative apparatus frequently accelerates dropouts through the denial of hall tickets, the diversion of teaching staff, and the generation of fraudulent tracking data. Addressing Karnataka's secondary dropout crisis requires moving beyond generic infrastructure improvements and directly confronting the harsh economic realities and systemic exploitation that pull adolescents out of the classroom.
